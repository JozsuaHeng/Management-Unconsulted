# Proposal / Statement of Work Drafter

## When to use this

The user needs a written proposal or SOW to send to a prospective or
existing client, defining what work will be done, for how much, and under
what terms.

## When NOT to use this

- The engagement scope isn't actually clear yet — clarify scope with the
  user (or note open questions explicitly in the draft) rather than
  inventing scope details that weren't discussed.
- This is a legal contract requiring specific jurisdiction/liability
  language — draft the business content, but flag that legal review is
  needed before it's a binding contract, not a substitute for one.

## Workflow

1. **Confirm what's actually known vs. assumed** before drafting: client
   name, problem being solved, deliverables discussed, timeline
   constraints, budget range (if known). Flag anything not yet confirmed
   rather than filling gaps with invented specifics.
2. **State the problem/objective first**, in the client's terms — what
   they're trying to achieve, not a list of activities. This frames
   everything that follows.
3. **Define scope explicitly, including what's out of scope.** Scope
   creep is the most common source of engagement friction — an explicit
   "not included" list prevents later disputes as much as the "included"
   list does.
4. **List deliverables as concrete artifacts**, not vague activities —
   "a written market-entry recommendation with supporting analysis," not
   "strategic advisory support."
5. **Lay out a timeline with milestones**, not just a single end date —
   makes progress checkable and sets expectations for when the client will
   see something.
6. **State fees and payment terms clearly**, including what happens if
   scope changes (a change-order or re-scoping clause) — this protects
   both sides and should be stated up front, not discovered mid-engagement.
7. **List assumptions and dependencies** — anything the engagement
   depends on the client providing (data access, stakeholder availability,
   timely feedback) that, if missing, would affect timeline or outcome.
8. **Flag that legal/contractual review is a separate step** if this
   proposal will become a binding agreement — this skill drafts the
   business content, not a vetted legal contract.

## Output format

```
## Proposal: [Engagement name] — [Client name]

### Objective
[What the client is trying to achieve, in their terms]

### Scope
**Included**: [...]
**Not included**: [...]

### Deliverables
1. [Concrete deliverable]
2. ...

### Timeline
| Milestone | Target date |
|---|---|
| ... | ... |

### Fees
[Fee structure, payment terms, change-order handling]

### Assumptions & dependencies
- [What the engagement assumes/depends on from the client]

### Open questions / to confirm before sending
[Anything not yet confirmed with the client]
```

## Common pitfalls

- Vague deliverables that can't be checked off as done or not done.
- No "not included" list, leaving scope open to drift.
- No change-order/re-scoping language, making later scope changes awkward
  to charge for.
- Inventing specifics (numbers, dates, names) that weren't actually
  discussed, instead of flagging them as open questions.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Objective is stated in the client's terms, not just a task list
- [ ] Scope includes both "included" and "not included"
- [ ] Deliverables are concrete, checkable artifacts
- [ ] Timeline has milestones, not just an end date
- [ ] Fees, payment terms, and change-order handling are stated
- [ ] Anything unconfirmed is flagged, not invented
