# Worked example: illustrative structure only

This is a generic, illustrative structure — not based on any real
client's actual tender content, which stays confidential to that
engagement.

Say a fictional consultancy is responding to an RFQ from a public-sector
agency for "strategic planning workshop facilitation" support, with
published evaluation criteria: (1) understanding of requirements — 20%,
(2) relevant experience — 30%, (3) proposed approach — 30%, (4) value
for money — 20%.

### Evaluation criteria cross-check (built early, drives the whole draft)
| Criterion | Weight | Addressed in |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding of requirements | 20% | "Understanding of requirements" section |
| Relevant experience | 30% | "Capability & experience" section |
| Proposed approach | 30% | "Proposed approach" section |
| Value for money | 20% | "Commercial offer" section |

Building this table **first**, before drafting prose, is what keeps the
response evaluator-focused rather than narrative-focused — every
section exists because a criterion demands it, not because it "seems
like the kind of thing a proposal should cover."

### Value proposition — weak vs. strong

Weak (generic, no evidence): "We are the best provider for this project
due to our extensive experience and outstanding value for money."

Strong (specific, evidence-backed): "We have facilitated [N] strategic
planning workshops for public-sector clients in the past three years,
including [comparable-scale example if disclosable], and our proposed
day rates sit [X]% below the panel median for this service category."

The difference is entirely about attaching a specific, checkable claim
instead of an unsupported assertion — this is what actually moves an
evaluator's score, not confident language.
