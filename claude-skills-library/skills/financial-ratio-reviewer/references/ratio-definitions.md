# Ratio definitions and plain-English framing

## Profitability

**Gross margin** = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) / Revenue
Plain English: "Of every dollar in revenue, how much is left after the
direct cost of delivering it, before overhead." For a charter business,
COGS is roughly the direct trip costs (fuel, provisioning, per-trip
crew if variable).

**Net margin** = Net Profit / Revenue
Plain English: "Of every dollar in revenue, how much is actually kept as
profit after everything — direct costs, overhead, and other expenses."

## Liquidity

**Current ratio** = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
Plain English: "Can short-term obligations (due within a year) be covered
by short-term assets (cash, receivables, etc.)?" Above 1 generally means
yes on paper; well below 1 is a flag worth naming plainly.

**Quick ratio** = (Current Assets − Inventory) / Current Liabilities
Plain English: like current ratio but excludes inventory (which can be
slow to convert to cash) — a stricter test of near-term coverage. Less
relevant for a service business with little inventory, more relevant for
retail/product businesses.

## Efficiency (use only if relevant to the business type)

**Receivables turnover** = Revenue / Average Accounts Receivable
Plain English: "How quickly customers actually pay what they owe" — low
turnover can signal collection problems even if revenue looks fine on
paper.

## General framing notes

- "Healthy" ranges vary a lot by industry — a service/charter business
  with little inventory naturally looks different from a retail business.
  State the caveat rather than applying a one-size-fits-all bar.
- A single period's ratios are less informative than a trend — always ask
  for or note whether a prior period is available for comparison.
- A ratio that's *too* high can also be worth flagging (e.g. an extremely
  high current ratio might mean cash is sitting idle rather than being
  reinvested) — don't assume higher is always better without context.
